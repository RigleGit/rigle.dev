Playing Balatro on my Nintendo DS

Rodrigo Iglesias // 2025-03-12 // 383 words // 2 min

Playing Balatro on Nintendo DS through an unofficial homebrew port: how it works, its limitations and how to try it with MicroLua. Includes a gameplay video.

Watch the video on YouTube (in Spanish).

If you spent hours playing Luigi’s casino minigame in Super Mario 64 DS, you might enjoy this: you can play Balatro on Nintendo DS thanks to an unofficial homebrew port. It is a cut-down adaptation, but seeing it run on the old dual-screen handheld is quite something.

In the video above, I show the project and how to try it. Here is the short version.

From Balatro to the dual screen

If you have not played it, Balatro combines deck building with poker hands. You make pairs, three-of-a-kind or straights to beat a target score, while Jokers change the rules and multiply your points. That description only goes so far: much of the appeal comes from finding combinations and watching the numbers climb.

Haynster brought that idea to Nintendo DS using MicroLua, an environment for running programs written in Lua on the console.

Update (26 October 2026): the original repository is no longer available, and I do not know why it was removed. For DS, you can use Grapeist’s fork. There is also Balatro3DS by idkhan, an adaptation for Nintendo 3DS with its own installation instructions.

What this Balatro NDS port includes

The version I show has 15 Jokers and 12 Boss Blinds. It lacks the original game’s music and different decks, and hand detection has limitations: a pair accompanied by three unrelated cards may not be recognised as a pair.

Even with those cuts, it is a fun project to try. The forks still let people look at how it works and contribute improvements.

How to try Balatro on Nintendo DS

You need MicroLua and the DS fork’s files:

  1. Download MicroLua 4.7.2 and place its lua folder at the root of your card.
  2. Copy the project folder into lua/scripts/.
  3. Launch MicroLua’s .nds file from your flashcard and open the project through its file browser.

You can also try it in an emulator. The fork’s README explains the DeSmuME setup, which needs access to the files through the Slot 2 flashcard option.

The DS was part of many people’s childhoods, and for some it was also a way into programming. That is why I enjoy seeing projects like this still appear. Between homebrew and romhacking, there are still plenty of reasons to turn it on again.
